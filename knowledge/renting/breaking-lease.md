## What it means to break a rental agreement

If a renter leaves the property before their rental agreement ends, or leaves without giving notice, they might be breaking their rental agreement. This used to be called breaking the lease.

If the rental provider (landlord) has to cover costs because the renter broke the rental agreement, the renter will usually have to pay them back. These costs might include remaining rent, advertising fees or fees to re-let the property. These are known as ‘lease-break fees’.

## Leaving early without paying costs

There are some reasons a renter can leave early without breaking the agreement and without having to pay costs. The renter must still use the proper form and give the minimum notice:

- the property does not meet minimum standards before the renter moves in: immediate
- a long-term agreement was verbal or did not use the right form: 28 days
- the rental provider refused changes needed to make the property suitable for someone with disability: 14 days
- the renter needs special and personal care, is moving into social housing, or needs temporary crisis accommodation (evidence required): 14 days
- the renter was given a Notice of intention to sell, unless the sale was disclosed before the agreement started: 14 days.

A renter can also leave early with 14 days’ notice after the rental provider gives them a notice to vacate (for major repairs, demolition, business use, the rental provider or family moving in, sale, public acquisition, or ending a fixed term). In these cases VCAT can decide lease break fees if appropriate.

## Costs for breaking an agreement

Renters do not have to pay a penalty for breaking the agreement, but they do have to cover certain costs so the rental provider doesn’t lose money. If the rental agreement lists fees for breaking it, it must give the reason for the fees.

Lost rent: VCAT decides how much the renter should pay and makes a compensation order. If the rental provider rents the property to someone else, the renter does not have to pay rent after the property is re-let. The rental provider must do everything they can to re-let the property quickly. For fixed-term agreements longer than 5 years, VCAT cannot order more than one month’s rent for every 12 months remaining, and never more than 6 months’ rent.

Advertising: the renter may have to cover reasonable costs the rental provider or agent paid to re-advertise the property.

Re-letting fee: if the rental provider uses an agent, the renter may be asked to pay the same proportion of the re-letting fee as the proportion of time left on the agreement. For example, if the re-letting fee is $500 and there are 6 months of a 12-month agreement left, the renter would pay half the fee ($250).

## Costs a renter doesn’t have to pay

Renters don’t have to pay:

- a penalty for breaking the agreement
- rent when there is a new renter in the property
- advertising fees when the property was not advertised
- re-letting fees if the property is rented directly with the owner and not through a real estate agent.

A renter still has the right to claim their bond after an agreement is broken.

## Disputing costs and hardship

If a renter thinks they have been charged too much for breaking the agreement, they should try to negotiate with the rental provider or agent, then apply to VCAT if that fails. A rental provider can also apply to VCAT to order a renter to pay costs.

If a renter is experiencing severe hardship, such as loss of income, a serious medical condition, or a family tragedy, they can apply to VCAT to break an agreement without having to pay any costs.
