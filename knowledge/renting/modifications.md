## Changes that can be made without permission

There are rules about the changes renters (tenants) can make to a property. They will often need permission from the rental provider (landlord). A rental provider may require that any changes are completed by a suitably qualified person.

A renter can install any of the following items without permission:

- non-permanent window film for insulation, reduced heat transfer or privacy
- a wireless doorbell
- curtains (but the renter must not throw out the original curtains)
- adhesive child safety locks on drawers and doors
- pressure mounted child safety gates
- a lock on a letterbox.

As long as the property is not listed in the Victorian Heritage Register, a renter can also install without permission:

- picture hooks or screws for wall mounts, shelves or brackets on all surfaces except exposed brick or concrete walls (do not screw, hammer or drill into asbestos-containing materials)
- wall anchors to secure items of furniture on all surfaces except exposed brick or concrete walls
- LED light bulbs which don’t need new light fittings
- low flow shower heads (the renter must not throw out the original shower head)
- blind or cord anchors
- hardware mounted child safety gates on walls other than exposed brick or concrete walls.

## Reasonable changes

For any other change, the renter must ask the rental provider’s permission. However, the rental provider cannot refuse permission for these changes unless they have a good reason:

- picture hooks, screws, wall anchors or hardware mounted child safety gates on exposed brick or concrete walls
- weather seals, and caulking or gap filling around windows, doors, skirting and floorboards
- a security system installed by a suitably qualified person that does not impact the privacy of neighbours
- flyscreens on doors and windows
- a vegetable or herb garden
- a secure letterbox
- painting of the premises
- modifications to secure external gates.

The rental provider also cannot refuse without a good reason changes that don’t penetrate or permanently change surfaces, fixtures or the structure; are needed for health and safety; are disability-related modifications an occupational therapist or other prescribed practitioner says are required; give access to phone, internet or television services; are reasonable security measures; are needed for the safety of a renter affected by family violence or protected by a personal safety intervention order; are needed so the renter is not too hot or cold; or are needed to reduce energy and water bills.

## Reasons a rental provider can refuse changes

Examples of where it might be reasonable to refuse include:

- the property has heritage protections that would be affected
- the changes would mean the property didn’t comply with other legal requirements
- the changes would significantly change the property
- the change would require modifications to other premises or common areas
- the change would result in additional maintenance costs if not reversed when the renter leaves
- reversing the changes is not reasonably practicable
- the property is about to be sold or vacated and the renter has been given a valid notice to vacate.

If the rental provider refuses, the renter should first ask why. If the renter thinks the rental provider is refusing reasonable changes, they can apply to VCAT for a ruling.

## Extra bond to cover changes

The rental provider may require the renter to pay an extra bond to cover the cost of undoing changes at the end of the rental agreement. This is called a modification bond. They cannot ask for it if the extra bond would be less than $500, if it is not reasonable compared to the cost of reversing the changes, if the rental provider does not want the changes reversed, or if the changes are funded by a scheme such as the NDIS that does not require the property to be restored.

## Unapproved changes and restoring the property

If a renter has made changes that the rental provider did not agree to, or that are not on the allowed list, the rental provider can give a written Notice of breach of duty requiring the renter to restore the property to its condition before the change, or pay the reasonable cost of restoring it.

In a short-term rental agreement (five years or less), apart from the allowed list, a renter must not make any changes without the rental provider’s permission. Even if a rental provider gives permission, they do not have to pay for the changes.

Before the end of a rental agreement, a renter who has installed fixtures or altered the property must restore it (allowing for fair wear and tear) or pay the reasonable cost of restoring it, unless the rental provider agrees otherwise, the rental agreement says so, or the renter paid a modification bond.

In a long-term agreement, changes agreed before signing and written into the agreement need no further permission. Later changes need the rental provider’s written permission.

## Safety devices

Renters must not remove, deactivate or interfere with safety devices, except where it is reasonable, for example dusting a smoke alarm or stopping it beeping after it accidentally went off. You must not take the batteries out to stop an alarm from working. Safety devices include smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, electrical safety switches, swimming pool barriers, fire safety equipment, and hot water safety devices.
