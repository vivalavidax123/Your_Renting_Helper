## What is a notice to vacate?

When a rental provider (landlord) wants a renter (tenant) to move out of the property, they can either talk to the renter to reach an agreement or give the renter a notice to vacate.

A notice to vacate is a formal statement that the rental provider wants to end the rental agreement. A rental provider can only give a notice to vacate for certain reasons, and must give the reason. They cannot give notice without a reason. A notice to vacate is not valid if it is unlawful discrimination.

If a renter does not leave by the termination date in the notice to vacate, the rental provider can apply to VCAT for an eviction or possession order.

A rental provider may ask a renter to leave without giving a notice to vacate. The renter does not have to agree to leave. If they do, they should record this in writing.

## Notice for doing something the law allows

A rental provider cannot give someone notice for doing something, or saying they will do something, they are legally allowed to do. For example, a rental provider cannot give a renter notice to vacate for requesting repairs, asking to have a pet, or challenging a rent increase.

## How a notice must be given

The notice must be addressed to the renter, give the reason the rental provider is ending the agreement, be signed by the rental provider (or their agent), be sent with enough time to reach the renter, and state the date the renter has to leave by (the termination date).

It must be delivered to the renter at the rented premises by registered post, by electronic communication such as email (only if the renter has consented to receive notices this way), or by hand.

## Challenging a notice to vacate

A renter can challenge a notice to vacate if they believe it was not given to them properly, disagree with the reason given on the notice, or were given the notice as a result of family violence.

## Reasons to end an agreement early and notice periods

A rental provider can only end a rental agreement early (before its stated end date) for one of the listed reasons. Otherwise the notice to vacate is not valid.

Immediate notice:

- the renter or their visitor intentionally or recklessly causes serious damage to the property, including safety equipment and common areas
- the renter or their visitor puts neighbours, the rental provider, the agent, or their contractors or employees in danger
- the premises are unfit for human habitation, destroyed totally, or destroyed to the extent that they are unsafe.

14 days’ notice:

- the renter or anyone living there seriously threatens or intimidates the rental provider, agent, contractors or employees
- the renter owes at least 14 days rent
- the renter has failed to comply with a VCAT compliance order
- the renter has already been given 2 breach of duty notices and the same breach occurs
- the property is being used for illegal purposes
- the renter has brought in other renters or sub-letters without consent
- the renter has not paid the bond as agreed
- a child under 16 lives at the premises when the rental agreement says this is not allowed
- social housing cases: the renter misled the housing authority, or drug-related activity in public housing.

28 days’ notice:

- the renter is keeping a pet without consent and VCAT has made an order excluding the pet.

## Notice at the end of a fixed-term agreement

If rental providers want renters to move out when an agreement ends, they still need to give notice for one of the listed reasons. The termination date must be on or after the end date in the rental agreement.

Since 25 November 2025, no fault evictions are banned: a rental provider cannot give a notice to vacate without a valid reason, even at the end of a fixed-term agreement. When a fixed-term agreement ends, it automatically becomes a periodic (month-by-month) agreement unless a new fixed-term agreement is signed or a notice to vacate is given for a valid reason. On a periodic agreement, a rental provider can issue a notice to vacate at any time, as long as it is for a valid reason.

## Reasons, evidence and notice periods at the end of an agreement

Some reasons need evidence attached to the notice. If required evidence is not supplied, the notice is invalid.

- The rental provider plans to move in at the end of the fixed term, and this was written in the agreement’s additional terms: 14 days. Evidence: the rental agreement and a witnessed statutory declaration with the date they intend to move in.
- Reconstruction, repairs or renovations that cannot go ahead unless the renter leaves, with permits obtained: 90 days. Evidence: photos and a tradesperson’s contract or quote explaining why the renter must leave and how long it will take, or a building permit.
- Demolition, with permits obtained: 90 days. Evidence: a demolition building permit and a contract with a builder-demolisher. The property must not be re-let as a residence for 6 months unless VCAT approves.
- The rental provider will use the property for something else, such as a business: 90 days. Evidence: a witnessed statutory declaration and an ABN, business registration or planning permit. No re-letting as a residence for 6 months.
- The rental provider, an immediate family member or a dependent will move in: 90 days. Evidence: a witnessed statutory declaration naming who will move in. No re-letting to anyone else for 6 months unless VCAT approves.
- The property is to be sold and vacated immediately after the agreement ends: 90 days. Evidence: a signed contract of sale, an agreement with a licensed estate agent to sell, or a contract of sale prepared by a conveyancer or lawyer. The agreement cannot be shortened for this reason, and no re-letting as a residence for 6 months unless VCAT approves.
- A government authority owns the property and needs it for public purposes: 90 days.
- Social housing, where the renter no longer meets the criteria: 90 days.

## Other ways an agreement can end early

If a rental provider is experiencing difficulty due to unforeseen circumstances, they can apply to VCAT to reduce or end a fixed term agreement. VCAT weighs the rental provider’s hardship against the renter’s.

A mortgagee (lender) such as a bank can give a renter a notice to vacate if they are repossessing the property.
